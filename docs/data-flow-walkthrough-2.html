Data Flow and Computation

An Explainable Career Category Recommendation System for Computer Science and Information Technology Graduating Students using Ensemble and Baseline Classifiers

Every transformation from raw questionnaire answer to final recommendation, with the formula computed at each stage and the real values for student R001 · 16 November 2026

This walkthrough follows one student, R001, through all twelve processing stages. For each stage it states where the code lives, the formula it computes, what that means in plain terms, and the actual numbers produced. Every figure below was recomputed from the current code and the saved model on 16 November 2026 — nothing here is illustrated or carried over.

What changed in this revision Stage 3 was rewritten. grade_to_points() no longer stretches the transcript linearly onto 0–100; it now interpolates the official PSU percentage equivalents (1.00 = 98–100%, 3.00 = 75–76%, 5.00 = below 75%). Because every academic feature is built on that function, every number downstream of it moved — domain scores, signals, RIASEC, EVT, the model's probabilities, SHAP values, alignment percentages and the ranked tracks. Figures and tables produced before 16 November 2026 are not comparable with the ones in this document.

Human: On the PSU scale 1.00 is excellent and 5.00 is a failure — lower is better. Feed that to a model and every SHAP plot reads backwards: it would say "high grades push you away from Technical." So grades flip to 0–100 points where higher always means better.

The point of this revision is which 0–100 scale. The function no longer invents a linear stretch; it reproduces the percentage equivalents printed in the PSU Undergraduate and Graduate Grading Scale, and interpolates between them for continuous values like a GWA. A 1.00 is now 99 points because the registrar calls it 98–100%, not 100 points because it happens to sit at the top of the range.

FormulaLet the official scale be the anchor pairs  (g_1, p_1) … (g_11, p_11)  from
PSU_GRADING_SCALE, where p_i is the MIDPOINT of the published percentage band:

    g :  1.00  1.25  1.50  1.75  2.00  2.25  2.50  2.75  3.00  4.00  5.00
    p : 99.0  96.0  93.0  90.0  87.0  84.0  81.0  78.0  75.5  72.0   0.0

Then for any grade g, clipped to [G_best, G_worst] = [1.00, 5.00]:

                                      p_{i+1} − p_i
    p(g)  =  p_i   +   ( g − g_i ) · ─────────────────        for  g ∈ [g_i , g_{i+1}]
                                      g_{i+1} − g_i

i.e. piecewise-linear interpolation through the official table (np.interp).
NaN in, NaN out — a missing grade is never silently scored as 0.

Two properties to know before you quote this Passing grades are compressed. Every mark from 1.00 to 3.00 now lands between 75.5 and 99 points, so academic features occupy the top quarter of the 0–100 range rather than spreading across it. Grade-based SHAP values therefore read in near-transcript units, which is easier to explain to a panelist, but the numbers are no longer comparable with any output produced before this revision. There is a cliff between 4.00 and 5.00. Interpolating from 72.0 down to 0.0 across one grade step means a 4.50 scores 36.0 — a slope six times steeper than anywhere in the passing range. No student in this cohort falls there, but a future record with a 4.00–5.00 mark will be penalised far harder than the scale itself implies. Flag it in Chapter V as a known property, not a bug.

Human: This is the most important design decision in the pipeline. BSCS students answered 17 courses, BSIT students 24, and they are different courses — so every BSIT column is blank for a BSCS student. If those 41 raw columns went to the model, a tree would learn to read the degree program off the pattern of blanks rather than off the grades.

Instead each course maps into one of 10 domains both programs share. "Data Structures" and BSIT's "Information Management" both land in domains the model understands the same way for everyone. A course may belong to more than one domain.

STAGE 5Competency signals — the heart of it

Code: features.py:compute_signal_matrix

Code_SIGNAL_GROUP_WEIGHTS = {"courses": 0.45, "skills": 0.40, "certs": 0.15}

weighted_total   = group_frame.mul(weights, axis=1).sum(axis=1, skipna=True)
available_weight = group_frame.notna().mul(weights, axis=1).sum(axis=1)
score = weighted_total.div(available_weight.where(available_weight > 0))

Human: 11 scores, each 0–100, each blending three kinds of evidence with deliberate weights: grades 45% (registrar-verified, trusted most), ticked skills 40% (self-reported), certificates 15%.

Those last two lines are subtle but critical. If a student skipped a question, only that evidence group drops out and the remaining weights re-normalise. A plain weighted sum would turn one blank cell into a NaN and silently wreck that student's entire report.

FormulaFor each signal s, three evidence groups are formed on a 0–100 scale:

    courses_s  =  mean over  d ∈ s.domains   of   acad_d
    skills_s   =  mean over  k ∈ s.skills    of   (x_k × 100)        x_k ∈ {0,1}
    certs_s    =  mean over  m ∈ s.certs     of   (cert_m × 100)

with group weights   W = { courses 0.45 , skills 0.40 , certs 0.15 }:

                 Σ over present groups g  of   W_g · value_g
    sig_s   =  ───────────────────────────────────────────────     clipped to [0, 100]
                 Σ over present groups g  of   W_g

THE DENOMINATOR IS THE POINT.  An absent group is removed from BOTH sums, so the
remaining weights renormalise. A plain Σ W_g·v_g would propagate one NaN into the
whole score — and from there into every alignment percentage for that student.
R001 — two signals worked in fullsig_programming                               ← all three groups present
   courses  0.45 × acad_programming(79.00)                        = 79.00
   skills   0.40 × mean(tech_programming=100, tech_web_mobile_dev=0) = 50.00
   certs    0.15 × mean(cert_programming=0)                       =  0.00
   = (0.45×79.00 + 0.40×50.00 + 0.15×0.00) / 1.00  =  55.55

sig_systems_engineering                       ← note the denominator
   courses  0.45 × mean(acad_systems_engineering=87.0, acad_foundations=93.0) = 90.00
   skills   0.40 × mean(hard_project_management=0, hard_research_writing=0)   =  0.00
            (no cert group is defined for this signal)
   = (0.45×90.00 + 0.40×0.00) / 0.85  =  47.65

The /0.85 is the renormalisation in action — this signal has no certificate component, so the remaining 0.85 of weight absorbs it instead of the score being diluted toward zero.

STAGE 8Into XGBoost

Code: models.py:build_models

Codexgb_steps = [("impute", SimpleImputer(strategy="median", keep_empty_features=True))]

Human: Not bare XGBoost — a three-step pipeline. Blanks filled with the column median, SMOTE balances the classes, then 400 shallow trees (depth 4) each correcting the last.

Why bundle them? If you SMOTE before splitting, synthetic students built from test-set neighbours leak into training and accuracy jumps 5–15 points for free — numbers that will not reproduce. Inside the pipeline, cross_validate refits imputation and SMOTE on each training fold alone. ZeroR deliberately receives no SMOTE: balancing its input would destroy the very floor it measures.

FormulaThe Shapley value of feature j — its average marginal contribution over all
orderings of the feature set F  (Lundberg & Lee, 2017):

                      |T|! · ( |F| − |T| − 1 )!
    φ_j  =     Σ     ───────────────────────────  ·  [ f(T ∪ {j}) − f(T) ]
            T ⊆ F\{j}            |F|!

TreeExplainer computes this exactly for tree ensembles in polynomial time.

That last check is your defense-proof moment. Lundberg & Lee's additivity property says the contributions must sum exactly to (margin − base value), and here they do to four decimals. A panelist can verify the arithmetic by hand. Nothing is approximated or hand-waved.

Read the contributions and the model's reasoning becomes legible — and partly uncomfortable. This is the 25% term at its most visible. Web Developer has the best raw competency fit of any track on the list (58.3, against 51.7 for the winner) and still ranks third, because the model gave Technical only 2.64% while giving Supervisory 90.13% — a 22.5-point swing in the model term against a 6.6-point advantage in the competency term. Two consequences worth stating in Chapter V. First, when the classifier is this confident, the 75/25 split stops behaving like a 75/25 split: the model effectively sorts by predicted category and competency only orders tracks within a category. Second, that confidence is the inflated training-row 90.13% from Stage 8 — so for students the model has memorised, the rule layer is quieter than the weights imply. Consider reporting category_probabilities = None alongside the ranked list, which scores on competency alone and would have put Web Developer first.
